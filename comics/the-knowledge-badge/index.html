At the crowded Proof & Prizes booth, William holds notes and raises a finger beside stacks of CHRISTIAN BELIEF pamphlets. Ruth gestures with an open hand beside a gold KNOWLEDGE badge on a stand marked SAMPLE. Phil, wearing his black cap and glasses, holds an open dark notebook and pen. Morgan watches beside him. Three balloons read from William at left, through Ruth at center, to Phil at right. Plush prizes, rides and passing visitors fill the open park behind them.

William: I defend Christian belief as possible knowledge, not merely a comforting guess. Warrant is the quality that, when sufficient and joined to truth, makes a belief knowledge. Conviction alone doesn't earn that status.

Ruth: As a Christian, I want that distinction. Strong belief can be mistaken. Does this badge certify the belief or merely congratulate the believer?

Phil: I'm not a believer. What makes a belief more reliable when we call it knowledge? Show us the reliable method. If the label merely rewards a firm answer, that's a promotion for confidence, not a promotion for the evidence.

Notes from the queue · 01

A title for the belief

William is defending a distinction between genuine knowledge and mere conviction. Phil asks what earns that distinction. A large badge makes the question easier to see; it does not answer it.

Warrant, in the account being discussed, concerns how a belief is formed. It can come in degrees. Knowledge requires truth and enough warrant; neither a confident feeling nor a printed label is sufficient.

A deeper dive

What is the badge supposed to certify?

Three different matters can be confused: whether a claim is true, how confidently someone believes it, and whether the way the belief was formed deserves trust. A person can be firmly convinced of something false. A person can also arrive at a true answer by luck. Calling either conviction or lucky correctness knowledge would leave the reliability question unanswered.

William uses warrant for the quality of a belief’s formation that, in sufficient degree and together with truth and belief, yields knowledge. On his account, properly working faculties, suitable conditions and successful production of true beliefs matter. This offers a substantive distinction, not merely a congratulatory word. Phil therefore needs to challenge the grounds for applying that distinction, rather than assume that every use of knowledge is empty.

A status claim is not additional evidence

Phil’s question concerns what changes when the title is awarded. If new reasons establish that a belief arose reliably, those reasons can improve our assessment. But printing a title while leaving the reasons unchanged supplies no such improvement. The difference is between recognizing a quality that is actually present and treating the act of recognition as if it created the quality.

The badge makes that danger visible without defining the philosophical theory by a novelty prize. Ruth wants knowledge to mark something more than enthusiasm; she is already asking a question a Christian can reasonably ask. Phil’s nonbelief likewise identifies his standpoint, not a premise proving that Christianity is false. The opening disagreement is about what earns an epistemic status: a standing a belief has as a candidate for truth.

Notes from the queue · 02

The prize rule

The game makes two different standards visible: what the evidence supports and what qualifies for a prize. Changing the second does not change the first.

The example assumes a fair, unseen draw from six circle and four triangle tokens. Every repeated draw returns the token and mixes the same ten again. Sixty percent is a stipulated chance in this toy setup; ninety percent is its invented prize threshold. Neither measures a religious claim.

A deeper dive

The chance and the reward are different rules

The setup gives a transparent basis for sixty percent: six of ten tokens carry a circle, and each token is assumed equally likely to be drawn unseen. For repeated trials, the drawn token is returned and the bag mixed again. These assumptions matter. A biased draw, a changed collection or information about the next token would require a different probability. No numerical probability for a religious claim is being smuggled into the example.

The prize requires a report of at least ninety percent followed by a circle. A report of sixty percent never qualifies, even though it accurately describes the stipulated chance. Moving the report to ninety percent makes a prize possible without adding information about the bag. The rule therefore creates an incentive to report more confidence than the evidence supports.

What the game does and does not model

A report is what someone announces. It need not equal private belief: a contestant could say ninety percent while privately remaining at sixty. The example directly exposes an incentive to overstate a probability. An argument that the same incentive changes a person’s actual conviction needs the further premise that people internalize the demanded report. That psychological step is possible, but it is not built into the arithmetic.

Ninety percent is an invented threshold, not a universal definition of knowledge or a requirement attributed to William’s full account. The game deliberately isolates a potential conflict between winning a status reward and accurately representing uncertainty. To apply that result to a theory of warrant, the critic must show that the theory creates the relevant conflict. Naming the prize KNOWLEDGE cannot establish that connection by itself.

Notes from the queue · 03

The missing conditions

William catches a real limitation in the analogy. A correct forecast and an emphatic report do not establish a reliable belief-forming process. His objection is part of the argument, not a concession to be hidden in small print.

The narrower criticism remains: if a rule pressures someone to exceed the support available, pursuing that rule can damage probabilistic accuracy. Whether the philosophical account imposes that pressure requires a separate argument.

A deeper dive

Why the missing conditions matter

A simplified model can reveal a real problem while failing to represent the theory it is meant to criticize. Here the prize rule includes a correct outcome and a sufficiently high report. It says nothing about whether the contestant’s faculties work properly, whether the conditions suit them, or whether the process is genuinely successful at producing truth. Those omissions remove precisely the features William regards as doing much of the philosophical work.

Warrant can also come in degrees. Falling short of the warrant needed for knowledge does not imply possessing none at all. Ruth’s sixty-percent assessment can therefore retain whatever warrant its basis supplies without becoming knowledge merely because she announces ninety percent. It would misrepresent the account to treat deliberate exaggeration as a method for manufacturing warrant.

The remaining criticism is conditional

Phil’s narrower argument has a clear structure. Suppose the support for a proposition stays fixed. Suppose a rule nevertheless rewards increasing reported or felt confidence beyond that support. Pursuing the rule can then conflict with accurately representing uncertainty. The game establishes an instance of that structure; the next scene calculates its cost.

Applying the conclusion to William’s account requires a bridge: evidence that its actual conditions impose the same pressure under the relevant circumstances. If proper function and successful truth-directedness instead require an accurate response to uncertainty, the game’s incentive may violate those conditions. The objection is not answered by repeating the word warrant, but neither is it established by omitting warrant’s conditions. Both sides owe an account of how the proposed process handles ambiguous support.

Ruth: Then ninety percent scores better if a circle appears. Doesn't that make the bolder answer better?

Phil: For that outcome, yes. But with a sixty-percent circle chance, expected loss is 0.24 for reporting sixty percent and 0.33 for reporting ninety. That's an average over possible outcomes, not a guaranteed win on each draw. The badge buys extra expected error. It doesn't buy a refutation of every knowledge theory.

Notes from the queue · 04

The cost of the promotion

The score is called Brier loss: square the difference between a reported probability and the outcome, recorded as 1 or 0. At an assumed circle probability of 0.6, reporting 0.6 gives expected loss 0.6 × 0.4² + 0.4 × 0.6² = 0.24. Reporting 0.9 gives 0.6 × 0.1² + 0.4 × 0.9² = 0.33. Lower is better.

A circle favors the 90% report on that draw; a triangle penalizes it much more. The comparison averages over those outcomes using the stated chance. It establishes an expected cost under this scoring rule, not the truth of one religious claim, a guaranteed finite-run result, or the falsity of every theory of knowledge.

A deeper dive

How the accuracy score works

Record a circle as y=1 and a triangle as y=0. Let q be the reported probability of a circle, expressed as a number between zero and one. The score is (q−y)²: subtract the outcome from the report and square the difference. This one-event version of Brier loss gives smaller scores to reports nearer the outcome. The squared difference penalizes a large confident mistake more heavily than a small one.

Ruth is correct about a circle. The sixty-percent report then scores (0.6−1)²=0.16, while the ninety-percent report scores (0.9−1)²=0.01. But a triangle reverses the advantage: the scores become 0.36 and 0.81. Looking only at the outcome that favors the bolder report would conceal its greater exposure to the other outcome.

Why the expected losses are 0.24 and 0.33

With a stipulated circle chance of 0.6, weight each possible score by its chance. Reporting 0.6 gives 0.6×0.16 + 0.4×0.36 = 0.24. Reporting 0.9 gives 0.6×0.01 + 0.4×0.81 = 0.33. The extra expected loss is 0.09. Expected means averaged across the possible outcomes using their probabilities; it does not promise that the better report will win on every draw or in every finite run.

More generally, if the circle chance is p, expected loss is p(1−q)²+(1−p)q² = p(1−p)+(q−p)². The first term does not depend on the report. The second is minimized at q=p. Raising q above the supported p therefore adds expected error under this scoring rule. A two-category convention that adds losses for both circle and triangle doubles these values but leaves the comparison unchanged.

The conclusion has a boundary

Every report at or above ninety percent has the same chance of earning this badge: the chance of a circle. Ninety percent is the least inaccurate qualifying report; the reward does not uniquely demand one hundred percent or the worst possible forecast. Nor does the calculation supply a measured record of calibration, which would compare many forecasts with outcomes. It shows the expected cost of crossing this stipulated threshold with unchanged support. Whether a philosophical theory demands an analogous move remains the separate question identified in the preceding scene.

Notes from the queue · 05

The feeling and its source

William gives the strongest version of the immediate-belief reply: a belief need not be inferred from other statements to be well formed. Phil accepts that point and asks about this particular identification of the experience's source.

Ruth's fictional report includes uncertainty. Neither the account being discussed nor the comic requires every religious experience to produce absolute certainty. Considering an experience as evidence still leaves room to ask what it supports and what might explain it.

A deeper dive

Immediate belief need not be a guess

Not every reasonable belief is reached by consciously assembling an argument. Seeing a counter ordinarily produces a belief that the counter is there without an internal probability calculation. William proposes that an awareness of the divine could also arise directly through a reliable faculty. If such a faculty exists, works properly and succeeds at producing true beliefs under suitable conditions, the absence of an accompanying verbal proof would not automatically disqualify its output.

This is a possibility claim with conditions. The familiar case of perception makes direct belief intelligible; it does not establish that every proposed faculty exists or is reliable. A further account is needed of why the religious case meets the relevant conditions. Likewise, describing an experience as immediate does not show that its interpretation is beyond scrutiny.

The experience and its explanation are distinguishable

Ruth can report that an experience seems like God’s presence while acknowledging that her conviction varies. Taking that report seriously does not settle its cause. The experience’s force is a fact about how compelling it is to her. Identifying its source requires considering what favors the divine explanation, what alternatives remain, and what evidence could increase or reduce trust in that identification.

Phil’s blank card represents the missing certificate of origin. It is not an argument that the experience has no evidential value. Experiences, their circumstances, their consequences and wider arguments may all enter an assessment, with their weight open to dispute. The central point is that strength of feeling and reliability of source are different questions. A strong feeling can accompany reliability, but its strength alone does not supply the distinction between a reliable process and a mistaken one.

The discussion addresses a proposed source of theistic belief. It does not pretend that every specifically Christian claim follows from a single experience, or that all religious believers report maximum certainty. Those stronger claims would go beyond the argument on the page.

A deeper dive

Reassurance is not a measure of reliability

The toy’s defect is stipulated and visible: the reservoir is empty while its indicator remains at FULL. An output that stays reassuring whatever the state of the tank fails as a fuel gauge. Giving that output a comforting purpose, or designing the device to issue it, does not make it sensitive to the fact it is meant to track.

The general lesson concerns a process’s relation to truth. A declared aim and successful performance are different. To assess a gauge, we ask whether its readings change appropriately with the contents, and under what conditions it fails. Comparable questions about a belief-forming process concern what connects its output to the relevant facts and what would reveal error.

William’s objection prevents a false victory

William’s account requires genuine success at producing true beliefs, not merely an intention to comfort or a label saying reliable. The broken toy therefore fails the requirement. Showing it to him cannot establish that the proposed religious faculty has the same defect. The analogy would become unfair if its conclusion were simply assumed in advance: religious experience is like a permanently stuck gauge, therefore it is unreliable.

Ruth instead asks for grounds to accept the favorable description. What supports the claim that the proposed faculty tracks truth rather than conviction alone? This leaves room for a response using experience, argument, corroboration or checks for error. A religious claim need not have exactly the same test as a mechanical reservoir to be assessable. But the absence of that exact test neither establishes reliability nor proves falsehood. The unresolved task is to explain what evidence is appropriate and why it supports the claimed connection to truth.

Notes from the queue · 07

Two certificates

William distinguishes a belief's actual standing from the person's ability to establish that standing. The distinction matters: a reliable process can operate without its user possessing a theory of its reliability.

Morgan presses a different question: what should guide an assessment when conflicting convictions present themselves as certain? Shared intensity does not decide which claim is true. Disagreement alone also does not prove both false or force equal odds.

A deeper dive

Possessing a quality and proving it differ

William distinguishes two questions: whether a process actually produces a warranted belief, and whether its user can demonstrate that fact. Those can come apart. Someone may see accurately without having an account of optics, or remember correctly without knowing how memory works. If warrant depends in part on the actual quality of the process, inability to produce a proof of that quality does not by itself show that warrant is absent.

This is often called an externalist view: some conditions of a belief’s good standing concern facts about its formation that need not be accessible to the believer’s own reflection. Phil’s question would miss the target if it silently redefined warrant as independently demonstrated warrant. The notebook keeps his practical question in view while William’s cards make the conceptual distinction explicit.

An assessor still needs grounds for a choice

A different question remains for someone deciding which disputed assurance to trust. Two incompatible claims can be held with equal intensity. That intensity cannot establish both claims, and it does not by itself identify which process is reliable. Morgan asks for reasons that discriminate between the competing claims: reasons whose force goes beyond each claimant’s declaration of certainty.

The hypothetical cards do not show that both claims are false, that the parties are equally reliable, or that the correct probability must be fifty percent. Relevant differences in evidence, method and track record might favor one side. Nor does disagreement automatically remove knowledge a person actually possesses. The point is narrower: shared certainty alone supplies no basis for selecting the reliable conviction. A printing press can reproduce a certificate more readily than an assessor can establish that it is deserved.

This challenge does not require escaping all cognition before using any faculty. It asks what comparisons, checks and arguments are available within inquiry, and how strongly they support the disputed claim. The possibility of unproved knowledge and the demand for responsible assessment can therefore both be taken seriously.

Leave room for the eraser

A challenge needs evaluation, not automatic surrender. An answer to a challenge also need not restore every lost degree of confidence. The relevant question is what the whole body of support now warrants.

Numbers are useful when defensible. A range, a qualified comparison or an honest statement of uncertainty can be more responsible than a precise-looking percentage with no basis. Experiences, explanatory arguments and checks for error can all be considered; their weight must be assessed rather than assumed.

A deeper dive

Revision works in both directions

Responsible confidence responds to the whole body of support. Favorable evidence can strengthen a belief; contrary evidence can weaken it. A title awarded earlier should not prevent either response. Ruth’s question concerns how a commitment remains answerable to reasons rather than becoming a rule that every future consideration must leave the conclusion untouched.

William is right that an objection requires evaluation. An unsupported disagreement is not automatically a reason to abandon a belief. An objection may rest on a false premise, misunderstand the claim, or be answered by evidence already available. Treating all challenges as decisive would be as unresponsive to their actual quality as treating none as relevant.

Answering an objection is not a blank cheque

Removing one reason for doubt does not automatically establish complete certainty. It may restore some lost support, reveal that a particular objection never had force, or leave other unresolved difficulties in place. The appropriate conclusion depends on what was challenged, how the answer works and what support remains independently of that exchange. A record of victories over isolated objections is not itself a measurement of the whole case.

The clipboard separates confidence from its support and from open questions so changes can be explained. Its blank columns deliberately avoid invented percentages. When the evidence does not warrant a precise number, an interval, a comparative judgment or a qualified statement of uncertainty may be more informative. That does not make every estimate equally good: the estimate still needs reasons, and its limits should be stated.

The eraser represents revisability, not habitual indecision. A person can act on a view while admitting conditions under which it should change. The scene neither claims that William’s account forbids objections nor implies that religious belief must always weaken under scrutiny. Its demand is that whichever direction confidence moves, the movement should be accountable to the assessed support.

A deeper dive

The dispute about knowledge remains real

William offers a reason to keep knowledge vocabulary: a true belief produced reliably differs from a lucky guess. A confidence number alone does not preserve that distinction. Two people can assign the same probability while using very different methods, and a confident falsehood does not become true because its confidence is precisely recorded.

Phil’s proposed replacement must therefore preserve truth, method, reliability and sensitivity to error, alongside the degree of confidence and its support. His position is that an explicit account of those matters is more useful in rigorous inquiry than a further status label that risks concealing uncertainty. This is a philosophical recommendation about how to conduct and describe inquiry. The token calculation does not force it, and William has not agreed to it.

Where the narrower argument ends

The mathematical lesson is that, under the stipulated chance and scoring rule, inflating a report from sixty to ninety percent increases expected loss. The reliability lesson is that a conviction’s intensity and a process’s reassuring description do not by themselves establish truth-directed success. Neither lesson demonstrates that knowledge is impossible or that every use of the word is misleading. A defender can retain the term while agreeing that it must not license unsupported confidence.

Ruth does precisely that. She keeps the badge as a label while refusing to treat it as new evidence or an answer to an objection. The assessment remains visible, and the eraser remains available. She has not abandoned her Christian belief or adopted Phil’s vocabulary. Their limited agreement is that a title cannot substitute for the work needed to justify the assessment beneath it.

The unfinished page leaves a continuing task rather than a manufactured conversion: state the claim, identify the support, examine the process and its possible failures, and explain what would change the assessment. The polemic targets unearned promotion. Whether knowledge talk can be used responsibly within that discipline remains the disagreement the characters carry away from the booth.

Calling a belief “knowledge” can sound like an upgrade. The skeptical question is what, exactly, has improved: the evidence, the method that produced the belief, or only the title attached to it? If a label rewards greater confidence without greater support, the promotion can make our assessment less accurate. A gold badge is an attractive accessory. It is a poor substitute for showing how the belief tracks truth.

The real target is an unearned upgrade

We should distinguish several things: whether a proposition is true, how confident a person is, what reasons support that confidence, and whether the process producing the belief is reliable. A lucky correct guess can be true without being a good method. A strong feeling can be sincere without being well supported. A reliable process may produce beliefs without its user being able to explain all its workings.

The proper-function account defended in the comic takes those distinctions seriously. Warrant is intended as a quality which, in sufficient degree and together with true belief, yields knowledge. The account requires properly functioning faculties, suitable conditions and a process successfully directed toward truth. It is not simply the instruction to feel certain. Warrant can have degrees, and confidence plus a lucky true answer does not exhaust its requirements.

The skeptical criticism must therefore be conditional. If a system rewards crossing a confidence threshold despite unchanged evidential support, it can conflict with accuracy. To establish that a particular theory of knowledge has this defect, one must show that the theory actually imposes that incentive. A toy booth can expose the problem clearly; it cannot make a sophisticated opponent responsible for a rule invented for the demonstration.

What the numbers establish

The practice game contains six circle tokens and four triangle tokens, with each token equally likely to be drawn. The probability of a circle is therefore 0.6. Reporting sixty percent expresses that probability. Reporting ninety percent exaggerates it. The booth's ninety-percent requirement is an invented prize rule, not an established universal threshold for knowledge.

To compare the reports, let a circle count as outcome 1 and a triangle as outcome 0. Subtract the outcome from the reported probability and square the difference. This is a form of the Brier score: smaller values represent smaller errors. A ninety-percent report scores better when a circle actually appears, but much worse when a triangle appears. The important comparison is the average error across the possible outcomes, weighted by their probabilities.

At a sixty-percent circle chance, reporting 0.6 gives expected error 0.6 × 0.4² + 0.4 × 0.6² = 0.24. Reporting 0.9 gives 0.6 × 0.1² + 0.4 × 0.9² = 0.33. The badge rule therefore rewards a report with greater expected error in this example. It does not guarantee a worse score on every individual draw; one lucky circle can flatter the exaggerated forecast.

The general result is equally revealing. If the event probability is p and the reported probability is q, expected squared error is p(1 − p) + (q − p)². The first term is unchanged by the report. The second is smallest when q equals p. Moving the report away from the supported probability increases expected error by the square of the discrepancy. The rule has made exaggeration profitable without making it accurate.

That result applies under the stated probability model and scoring rule. It does not prove that every categorical concept of knowledge demands exaggeration, that every sufficiently confident belief is irrational, or that all reliability conditions reduce to one score. In difficult religious disputes, the evidential probability may itself be uncertain. The lesson is to represent that uncertainty honestly, not to invent decimals and then mistake their precision for justification.

The experience and its cause are different questions

A believer may say that awareness of God is immediate, like perception, rather than the conclusion of an explicit argument. Immediate belief need not be irrational. We do not ordinarily construct a proof before seeing a chair, and a reliable faculty could in principle produce a justified belief before its user understands its mechanism. Skepticism gains nothing by pretending that every reasonable belief must begin with a written syllogism.

The question is whether the proposed religious faculty actually has the relevant connection to truth. A compelling experience supplies information about what the person experiences; it does not automatically identify the experience's cause. Interpreting it as awareness of God adds a claim that must be assessed. Intensity alone cannot settle the matter, especially when similarly intense experiences receive incompatible interpretations.

The stuck fuel gauge illustrates the distinction between a confident output and a reliable process. A gauge that always reads FULL fails because its output does not respond appropriately to the tank. Merely saying that it was designed to give that reading cannot repair the failure. But the analogy does not establish that religious experience is a stuck gauge. A proper-function theory requiring successful truth-directed operation would reject the defective gauge too. The remaining task is to investigate whether the alleged divine sense meets the conditions claimed for it.

Having reliability is different from establishing it

A person could possess knowledge without being able to prove to an audience that the belief-forming process is reliable. This is a serious point. The skeptical demand for reasons must not silently become the stronger claim that no one can know anything without first independently certifying every faculty involved. That demand would threaten ordinary perception and memory along with religious belief.

Yet the distinction does not settle a dispute between rival claims to religious authority. When we are asked to accept a belief or increase our confidence, the claim that its production would be reliable if the proposed religious account were true does not itself establish that the account is true. A possible explanation of how someone could know leaves the public question of what supports accepting that explanation.

Conflicting certainties make that question urgent without proving that every claimant is mistaken. One claimant might possess a reliable process while another does not. What distinguishes them? Relevant evidence could include coherence, consequences, independent arguments and comparison with alternative explanations. Assessing a unique claim does not require literally stepping outside the universe or conducting repeated universe-sized experiments. It does require reasons capable of doing more than reproducing the claimant's assurance.

Keep the eraser beside the conclusion

Evidence can support a belief, undermine it or answer an earlier objection. The strength of confidence should remain responsive to those changes. Successfully answering one objection does not automatically remove every other uncertainty, just as one unresolved difficulty does not necessarily defeat a well-supported overall case. The assessment needs to preserve how much support remains.

A knowledge vocabulary can coexist with these distinctions. Its defenders can reasonably argue that it records truth, reliability and the difference between success and luck. The skeptical preference here is to make those features explicit rather than let the word “knowledge” obscure them. In rigorous inquiry, an account of the claim, the reasons, the method and the remaining uncertainty is more informative than a ceremonial announcement that the badge has been earned.

The case for dispensing with the label in such assessments is therefore methodological, not a theorem proved by the toy game. Retain every defensible truth and reliability condition. Retain the evidence and revise the confidence. What should be discarded is any supposed epistemic promotion for which no improvement in support or truth-tracking can be shown. The clipboard tells us what can be defended. The badge tells us what somebody would like to call it.
