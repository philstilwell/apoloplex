The Trust Fall

A small trust fall raises a larger question: what gives a promise its credibility?

When is trust justified? A philosophical comic examines childlike faith, testimony and religious authority, with objections and skeptical analysis of evidence.

At the Grand Midway, Greg stands at a low stone plinth with a closed book and open palm beside a QUESTIONS WELCOME board. Ruth holds her cream tote and opens her other hand toward Phil. Phil touches his pointed goatee while holding an open notebook. Behind them, two teal-shirted attendants wait beside a low blue platform, thick teal mat and TRUST FALL sign. The APOLOPLEX arch, ticket kiosks, rides and busy park visitors fill the background.

Greg: I invite people to trust God's promise of eternal life through Jesus. By 'childlike,' I mean humble and willing to learn, not believing anything you're told. Trust can rest on reasons.

Ruth: I'm a Christian. My children trust me because I've cared for them. They don't need a new investigation every time I say I'll pick them up.

Phil: I'm not a believer. Your children have good reason to rely on you because you've consistently cared for them. What gives me reason to believe that God made this particular promise? Calling my posture childlike doesn't identify the promise's source.

Notes from the queue · 01

What does childlike mean?

A familiar word can hide different requests. Greg first gives “childlike” its strongest reasonable meaning; Phil asks what supports the particular promise being offered.

Humility, receptivity and dependence are not equated with gullibility. The Christian promise is stated as Greg’s belief, not established narration.

A deeper dive

The word does not settle the rule

Childlike can describe humility, curiosity, dependence or willingness to receive help. None of those attitudes requires accepting everything a speaker says. A criticism that quietly substitutes gullibility for humility would miss Greg's stated position. The relevant question is what the recommendation requires when a disputed claim is examined.

Phil therefore asks about a particular assertion: that God offers eternal life through Jesus. Willingness to listen creates an opportunity to consider reasons. It does not identify who made the promise or show that the promise is reliable. The missing work concerns the message, not the emotional attractiveness of the listener's posture.

The parent analogy begins with a real strength

Ruth's children have an accumulated history with her. They need not reconstruct that history every time they rely on her. Much reasonable trust works through remembered interactions rather than a fresh formal argument. The episode accepts this strength before asking how far it extends.

The criticism is conditional: if childlikeness names openness to reasons and correction, it is compatible with mature trust. If it becomes a reason to stop examining the message's credentials, the invitation has changed. Calling the second demand humble does not give it the evidential support of the first.

Greg stands at left with one open palm extended toward a small blue platform. Alex stands on the ground at right, hands in his gray hoodie pockets with a notebook tucked in. A thick teal mat lies on the ground behind the platform. Two teal-shirted attendants wait with open hands on either side; a smiling participant walks away between them, giving a thumbs-up. A large TRUST FALL board, toy stall, rides and crowds fill the midway.

Greg: Let's start with trust we can see. Would you let yourself fall backward from that low platform, trusting those two attendants to catch you?

Alex: I'm a nonbeliever, and I would consider it. I've watched these two catch three adults from this low platform. They're ready, and there's padding. Those observations give me reasons to expect another catch, though they don't guarantee it.

Greg: So trust doesn't require certainty before you act.

Alex: Agreed. But removing certainty doesn't remove the need for reasons. Watching those catches supports this small fall with these attendants. A reassuring invitation alone wouldn't give me the same support.

Notes from the queue · 02

Reasons to lean back

Alex can rely on the attendants without pretending that three successful catches make failure impossible. The evidence and the remaining uncertainty coexist.

Three prior catches are fictional observed events reported by Alex. They are not a measured failure rate or an adequate basis for universal safety claims. This is a fictional supervised demonstration, not activity instructions.

A deeper dive

Reasons survive the loss of certainty

Alex describes specific observations: the same two attendants have caught adults from the same low platform and are now prepared to do so again. Those observations bear on the next catch because the people, task and conditions are relevantly similar. They supply support rather than a guarantee; three successes cannot reveal every possible failure.

The padding and low height also affect the consequences of participating. Reasons for expecting success and reasons for accepting a remaining risk are related but different. A decision can be reasonable without the participant assigning certainty to its outcome.

What the invitation adds

A reassuring invitation may express willingness and help coordinate the action. It does not replace evidence of competence. The phrase 'trust me' is a claim about the speaker's reliability, and its weight depends on what else is known about that speaker and task.

Greg is right that action need not wait for certainty. Alex's reply rejects a further inference: because certainty is unnecessary, supporting reasons must also be unnecessary. The scene establishes neither a probability for the catch nor anything about divine action. Its narrow point is that reasonable trust can remain answerable to evidence while enabling action.

Ruth and Phil talk beside a wooden park bench beneath the Apoloplex gate. Ruth holds one hand to her chest and opens her other palm toward Phil; a cream tote with a family photograph rests on the bench. Phil opens one palm in reply while holding a small black notebook. Behind them, two attendants stand on the paving beside a teal landing mat, separated from the low blue platform. Four white speech balloons alternate between Ruth and Phil.

Ruth: My children have years of dependable care to go on, not just three catches. Why shouldn't they also trust the Christian faith I've taught them?

Phil: Your care gives them reason to expect care, and your honesty gives your testimony weight. But being loving and honest doesn't by itself show that you know what happens after death. We still need the reasons behind that teaching.

Ruth: So my testimony counts, but its weight depends partly on how I could know about eternal life?

Phil: Yes. Trusting Christian or Muslim parents due to their dependable caregiving does not legitimate defaulting to their favorite God.

Notes from the queue · 03

A parent's reach

A good reason to trust someone in one role is not an unlimited license for every claim they repeat. Honesty matters, but honest people can pass on mistaken information.

No allegation that Ruth lies or that her faith is false follows. The question is what supports this particular testimony beyond her dependable care.

A deeper dive

Care, honesty and knowledge do different work

Dependable care gives a child substantial reason to rely on a caregiver. Honesty is also relevant to testimony: a truthful person is less likely to knowingly mislead. Neither feature by itself establishes access to every fact. A person may sincerely repeat an inaccurate claim, or possess excellent judgment in one area without corresponding knowledge in another.

The phrase 'by itself' matters. Phil does not declare that Ruth has no additional reasons for Christianity. He asks for them. The conclusion is that a history of care cannot simply be transferred, unchanged, into authority about life after death. The claim's subject determines which competence and information are relevant.

Dependence is not the defect

Children need help before they can assess every source, and adults remain dependent on others throughout life. It would be absurd to require a child to verify every parental warning independently. Responsible reliance can begin provisionally and become better informed as experience and understanding develop.

Ruth's follow-up states the positive standard: testimony counts, while its weight depends partly on how the speaker could know. Trust is therefore differentiated rather than abolished. Discovering a limit to someone's knowledge need not erase confidence in their care, honesty or genuine expertise.

Alex: Exactly. I'm comparing reliance on a trusted adult, not claiming equal evidence for the beliefs. We must examine the reasons for each. Learning Christianity from a parent neither proves nor refutes it.

Notes from the queue · 04

The story a trusted person tells

The comparison concerns how a belief reaches a person. A shared route of transmission does not give different beliefs the same truth value or the same evidence.

The family is explicitly hypothetical. No universal account of children’s development, Santa belief or parental conduct is claimed.

A deeper dive

A channel is not a verdict

The hypothetical parent transmits a sound road-safety warning and a false Santa story. This shows only that being told something by a trusted person is not a guarantee of truth. It does not show that testimony is worthless: a generally reliable channel can still carry errors, and different messages can have very different supporting evidence.

Ruth's objection prevents the analogy from doing illegitimate work. Christianity's philosophical, historical and experiential arguments cannot be disposed of by placing a Santa hat beside them. Alex accepts that limit. The relevant comparison is reliance on a trusted adult before separately assessing the particular teaching.

Origin explains uptake; support determines confidence

Learning a belief in childhood can help explain why it feels familiar or compelling. That explanation does not establish whether the belief is true. Rejecting a belief solely because of its origin would be as careless as accepting it solely because a beloved person taught it.

Correction should also be proportionate. Evidence that a parent sustained one fictional tradition need not show that the parent is unreliable about traffic or family routines. The practical task is to identify what went wrong, which claims are affected and whether relevant reasons should raise or lower confidence. That task applies to inherited skeptical beliefs as well as inherited religious ones.

Notes from the queue · 05

Who made the promise?

“A perfectly reliable source should be trusted” is a conditional claim. It does not identify a contested message as coming from that source.

The scene does not deny that arguments might support the texts. It rejects using childlike deference as a substitute for those arguments. Greg’s caution about private revelation is retained.

A deeper dive

The conditional is granted

If a perfectly knowledgeable and trustworthy God actually made a promise, that promise would have a different standing from an uncertain human prediction. Phil grants this. The disagreement concerns whether the condition has been established: did such a source make this particular promise, and do the available texts and interpretations convey it accurately?

Moving from perfect reliability if correctly identified to correct identification merely because reliability is claimed leaves a premise missing. A message does not authenticate its own divine origin simply by describing its source as infallible. An additional case could supply support, but the invitation to trust cannot stand in for that case.

Human mediation remains visible

Most public religious messages reach an audience through speakers, documents, translations and interpretations. Identifying those links is not a debunking trick. It makes the actual claim assessable: what did a source report, how could it know, and what supports the interpretation being offered? Human participation is compatible with truth, but it prevents us from silently treating the entire route as error-free.

Greg expressly offers to defend the texts with reasons and rejects treating every private feeling as God's voice. This preserves a meaningful Christian position. The unresolved work is the promised defense. The scene neither undertakes the entire historical case nor announces that the case must fail; it shows why the adjective childlike cannot complete it.

Notes from the queue · 06

More than one kind of evidence

A past event cannot always be repeated. That does not make every report equally credible: access, accuracy and independent support still matter.

The report about yesterday is introduced with “Suppose.” Testimony’s possible value is conceded without judging particular Christian historical claims.

A deeper dive

The evidence must fit the claim

Greg correctly objects to making repeated demonstrations the universal model of knowledge. A historical event may be unique, and a credible witness can provide evidence of it without arranging a repeat. The trust-fall comparison would become misleading if its physical repeatability were imposed on every religious or historical assertion.

Phil offers a hypothetical report to make the alternative concrete. Opportunity to observe matters because it connects the witness to the event; a record of accuracy bears on the reliability of the report; genuinely independent corroboration reduces the chance that apparent agreement merely repeats one mistake. These are relevant considerations, not a mechanical formula or a demand that every report possess all three equally.

The conclusion needs its own support

A report can warrant some confidence without guaranteeing certainty. Its scope also matters. Evidence that a narrator accurately describes one event need not establish every interpretation or further proposition attached to the story. Additional claims require appropriate connecting reasons.

The episode leaves specific Christian historical arguments for their own assessment. That is a limit of scope, not a concession that no such evidence exists. The point here is that childlike trust cannot replace the work of assessing testimony, and the skeptic cannot evade that work by insisting that only repeatable experiments count.

Notes from the queue · 07

The comfort and the cause

The comfort of an experience and an explanation of that comfort are different claims. Respecting the experience does not settle which explanation is best.

Ruth’s experience is invented dialogue, not a claim about any real person. The scene does not deny noninferential personal reasons or establish a natural cause.

A deeper dive

Value and explanation can come apart

Ruth reports comfort and a lived sense of care. The episode grants that these matter deeply. But hope can arise from true and false beliefs alike, so the hope itself does not show whether the belief is true. A broader theory of personal religious experience is not decided by that limited point.

A further question arises when the experience is offered to identify a particular unseen agent or establish a promise about life after death. Feeling cared for and being cared for by that agent are different propositions. Memory, hope and community are possible alternative contributors, not established explanations of Ruth's particular experience. Phil asks how to compare them; he does not pretend to have already settled the comparison.

Personal trust and persuasion

Some accounts of religious belief allow a person to have warrant without deriving belief from public arguments. This episode does not settle those accounts. Its point is that the emotional benefit of a belief alone does not establish its truth, identify the cause of an experience, or complete the further task of persuading someone who does not share the interpretation.

Ruth can retain her belief while acknowledging that she has not established the comparison for Phil. Phil must equally acknowledge that an unanswered comparison is not a disproof of divine care. The bounded skeptical conclusion is that the experience's emotional value alone cannot identify its cause or establish every religious promise associated with it.

Notes from the queue · 08

The same question elsewhere

The same standard can yield different judgments when evidence differs. Sincerity on both sides cannot, by itself, choose between contradictory teachings.

The two communities are hypothetical, and no equal-evidence or equal-probability claim is made. The rule also constrains skeptical dismissal.

A deeper dive

Compare the reasons, not the warmth of the invitation

Greg's objection is substantial: traditions may offer different arguments, testimony and explanations. Fair comparison does not require assigning every tradition the same credibility. It requires identifying the relevant differences and explaining why they support different confidence.

Alex's hypothetical makes the disagreement explicit. One community teaches life after death; the other says death ends everyone's life. Those teachings cannot both be true in the same sense, while sincere parents and devoted believers can occur in both communities. Sincerity alone therefore cannot select the true teaching. This does not make sincerity irrelevant to honesty or make the teachings equally probable.

The skeptic is subject to the same discipline

A critic who dismisses testimony simply because it is religious would avoid the comparison rather than conduct it. Access to the reported facts, relevant reliability, corroboration and counterevidence should be considered even when the result threatens a favored skeptical belief.

Portable standards are standards one is prepared to apply when the roles are reversed. They do not prohibit different outcomes; they require a relevant explanation for those outcomes. Missing numerical probabilities do not license invented equality. The episode asks for defensible reasons for unequal confidence, not a universal tie between all worldviews.

Notes from the queue · 09

Willing to trust whom?

Willingness to trust a promise if it is true and its maker is good does not establish either condition. Rational confidence must track the relevant evidence; a possible attitude problem is not a measure of that evidence.

Frank explicitly allows exceptions. This fictional exchange is not a real quotation or evidence of his motives. Ruth does not deny that motives can distort inquiry.

A deeper dive

The conditional question has a limited use

Asking whether someone would accept Christianity if it were true may reveal a practical or emotional obstacle worth discussing. It does not show that Christianity is true, and a response to that hypothetical cannot establish the divine origin of a particular promise. Alex answers affirmatively while keeping the original evidence question open. Alex also makes reconciliation with the alleged God's goodness a condition of trust. That is a separate moral qualification, not evidence that the promise was made; the present scene does not resolve it.

The same distinction appears at the platform. Willingness to participate does not identify a competent catcher. A person may be willing in principle while checking the conditions on which that willingness depends. The checking is not logically inconsistent with trust.

A character possibility does not answer a factual question

Frank notes that a proud person could use endless demands to evade a conclusion, and Ruth concedes the possibility. She then states the positive standard: rational belief is graded, and its degree should correspond to the strength of the evidence. A willingness test cannot replace that assessment. The exchange does not claim to diagnose the motives of any particular doubter.

Even a demonstrated flaw in the questioner's attitude would not establish the promise. The reasons offered for that promise must still be assessed. Intellectual humility includes admitting one's own limitations, but also admitting that another person or institution may have exceeded its knowledge. The standard applies equally to believers and skeptics.

Notes from the queue · 10

A course with one permitted answer

A course can honestly teach a position. It cannot treat a required conclusion as evidence that an open examination succeeded.

The church and rule are hypothetical. Staying Christian after study, consulting mentors or teaching doctrine is not itself evidence of intellectual closure.

A deeper dive

Teaching and testing have different aims

A community may teach its doctrine, explain its arguments and encourage members to consider difficulties patiently. Beginning with commitments is unavoidable, and remaining convinced after investigation does not prove that the investigation was dishonest. Greg rightly insists on this distinction.

Ruth's hypothetical introduces a different rule: continued doubt is automatically classified as insufficient humility. Phil identifies that exact rule rather than accusing every church of operating it. The problem is that the process's permitted endpoint has been fixed before the force of the reasons is assessed.

What an open result requires

If relevant support weakens, an honest assessment may leave someone less confident or unconvinced. Declaring that outcome a defect of character prevents the process from acknowledging one possible result of the examination. More reading can occur within such a rule, but the amount of reading does not establish openness.

This does not require abandoning a worldview at its first anomaly. Prior support and plausible replies may justify patience. The requirement is that contrary evidence have some genuine capacity to matter, including to central claims when appropriate. The criticism concerns a protected conclusion, not every form of religious instruction, and it applies to secular groups that impose the same restriction.

Trust with your eyes open

A person can decide to act without a guarantee while keeping belief proportional to the evidence. Having some reasons is not a license for unlimited confidence.

No certainty, exact risk calculation, proof or disproof of God, or endorsement of a religious promise follows from the demonstration. The decision to try and the confidence in success remain distinct.

A deeper dive

Action does not require pretending to know

Alex judges that the observed performance and present conditions are enough for him to participate in this small demonstration. His decision does not require claiming that failure is impossible. Practical choices depend on both expectations and stakes; neither a willingness to act nor a desire for an outcome automatically raises the probability that the outcome will occur.

A Christian may likewise describe faith as entrusting oneself rather than merely affirming a sentence. That description is worth preserving. It does not remove the factual questions involved in the act: whom is one trusting, what has that agent promised, and what reasons support identifying the promise? Action and belief can be related without becoming interchangeable.

The conclusion stays within its evidence

Greg agrees that trust can have reasons, and Phil adds the stricter requirement that the degree of belief match the strength of the relevant evidence. The demonstration itself settles neither God's existence nor whether God promised eternal life. It illustrates reasonable reliance in an ordinary situation. A demand for childlikeness cannot replace relevant support for the religious promise or make contrary evidence inadmissible.

Humility is compatible with confidence when reasons are strong and with reduced confidence when reasons weaken. It is also compatible with temporarily withholding judgment. Alex chooses to try after checking the conditions, without pretending that a guarantee follows. Any authority claiming to welcome inquiry should make room for the possibility that an answer changes the listener's confidence.

After the ride · The skeptical case & private feedbackTrust deserves reasons, not a return to childhoodOpen the article and feedback formClose the article and feedback form · 6 min read

There is nothing admirable about being impossible to teach. We depend on parents, witnesses, teachers and people who know more than we do. The skeptical objection to childlike faith therefore cannot be that trusting anyone is foolish. It concerns a much more specific demand: accept a contested religious message with less scrutiny, and regard your reluctance to do so as a failure of humility.

That demand trades on the good reputation of trust while evading the question that makes trust reasonable. Who is asking to be believed, what are they claiming, and how could they know? A warm invitation does not answer those questions. Neither does the word childlike. When the message concerns a divine promise, the importance of getting it right increases the need to examine its credentials.

A parent can earn trust without becoming infallible

A child may have excellent reasons to rely on a familiar caregiver. Meals arrive, help comes, warnings prove useful and promised pickups happen. Much of that history is learned through ordinary life rather than explicit argument. Requiring a fresh proof before every act of reliance would make learning and cooperation needlessly difficult. Adults also cannot personally verify everything they need to know.

But reasonable reliance has a scope. A parent's care supports expecting care. Honesty makes a report worth taking seriously. Neither establishes unlimited knowledge. A sincere parent can pass on a mistaken teaching, just as an expert can make an error outside the field in which the expertise was earned. The question is not whether the person deserves love. It is whether this particular assertion has the support being claimed for it.

The Santa comparison is useful only at this limited level. A trusted channel can carry accurate warnings and a fictional tradition. That fact does not make Christianity a fictional tradition, give the two beliefs equal probabilities, or discredit everything learned from parents. It shows why the origin of confidence cannot serve as a universal certificate of truth. The inherited belief still needs assessment on its merits.

Calling the source divine does not identify it

The strongest Christian reply is that God would differ categorically from a fallible parent. If a perfectly knowledgeable and reliable God made a promise, that promise would deserve trust. A skeptic can grant this conditional without granting that a particular text, preacher or interpretation communicates such a promise.

The distinction is elementary but decisive. 'A perfect source would be reliable' does not establish 'this message comes from a perfect source.' The latter claim needs support. In public religious discussion, messages arrive through people, documents, traditions and interpretations. Those routes may convey truth. Naming them does not refute the message; it reveals where claims about access, reliability and meaning must be examined.

Additional historical or philosophical arguments might provide that support. They deserve to be assessed. What cannot provide it is a demand that the hearer already adopt the trust appropriate after the source has been identified. Otherwise incompatible communities can each certify their own authority by asking outsiders to become more receptive to it. The disputed conclusion has merely been moved into the entrance requirement.

Evidence is broader than an experiment

A trust-fall demonstration makes some reasons visible: the people, their preparation and their previous performance. Its usefulness ends if we insist that every rational belief must be supported by a repeatable physical performance. Reports of past events can be credible even when the events cannot be repeated. Testimony is often evidence, not an embarrassment that rational adults must eliminate.

That does not make all testimony equally credible. We can ask whether the speaker had access to the event, whether the source is accurate in the relevant domain, whether apparent corroboration is independent, and what contrary information should matter. No single checklist guarantees truth, but these questions connect confidence to features that can make a report more or less reliable.

A cumulative Christian case should face the same discipline as any other case. Sincerity, antiquity or emotional conviction should not receive special weight at home while being dismissed out of hand elsewhere. Conversely, the skeptic cannot dismiss a report merely because it is religious. Different confidence is justified by relevant differences in support, not by a preferred label.

A meaningful relationship still includes factual claims

Religious faith can be lived as a relationship, and an experience of care can be personally important. The critic need not deny that importance or demand that every moment of personal trust be reconstructed as a formal proof. Some accounts of religious warrant also allow belief to be reasonable without being inferred from a public argument. The present criticism does not settle those accounts.

Still, the claim that a particular unseen agent provided comfort is not identical to the claim that comfort occurred. Nor does comfort itself establish a promise of eternal life. An outsider can respect the experience while asking what supports its interpretation. Memory, hope and community are possible alternatives to consider, not explanations a skeptic is entitled to declare true without investigation.

Personal entitlement to trust and successful public persuasion are therefore different tasks. A believer may retain confidence while acknowledging that she has not established the cause of an experience for someone else. The outsider may withhold assent without claiming to have disproved divine action. This is a real disagreement about support, not an insult to the experience.

Humility must allow the messenger to be wrong

Pride can distort inquiry. So can fear, social pressure, loyalty and the wish to preserve a familiar identity. None of these possibilities belongs exclusively to believers or skeptics. But diagnosing a possible attitude does not answer a request for evidence. A proud person and a careful person can ask the same question; distinguishing them requires more than disliking the question.

Even if a particular critic is arrogant, the religious message still needs support. A willingness question has similar limits. Someone might agree to trust God if convinced that God made a promise, while remaining unconvinced that the promise has that source. The willingness is conditional on the fact in dispute. It cannot establish that fact.

A study process becomes especially suspect when continued doubt automatically counts as inadequate humility. Teaching a tradition is legitimate, and remaining convinced after study is not proof of a rigged result. The defect appears when renewed assent is the only permitted evidence of responsible inquiry. A process that cannot acknowledge weakened confidence has restricted the result while advertising the activity as open examination.

The right to revise is part of trust

Mature trust does not require discarding a person or worldview after one mistake. Strong prior support can justify patience, and explanations of apparent failures may be sound. But contrary information must have somewhere to register. We should be able to say which claims it affects and why confidence should rise, fall or remain unchanged. A standing exemption from correction is not patience; it removes an authority from assessment.

Acting under uncertainty is likewise different from manufacturing certainty. A person may choose to take a small risk, continue a relationship or investigate a religious practice without claiming that the evidence is stronger than it is. Decisions depend partly on stakes and values. Those considerations can explain action without becoming additional proof that its factual assumptions are true.

The trust fall proves no theological verdict. Its point is smaller and harder to evade: looking for reasons can be part of trusting well. Childlikeness is defensible when it means curiosity, receptivity or freedom from arrogance. When it means protecting a disputed message from adult scrutiny, the language of virtue is doing a job that belongs to evidence. A promise does not become more credible because its listener has been persuaded to ask fewer questions.
