Episode 02 / Carousel of Certainty

The Confidence Booster

A sideshow can raise a needle in seconds. Phil asks what else has changed.

An argument takes a day out

Frank, Greg, Jonathan, Phil, Morgan and Ruth
Belief, commitment, and evidence

Original fictional satire. All dialogue and anecdotes are invented; these are imagined conversations, not actual encounters or quotations.

Notes from the queue · 01

More confidence while you wait

The booth has excellent control over its needle and no control over the resurrection. Frank’s invitation raises a real distinction: relying on someone is an action; judging a historical claim is an assessment of what happened.

The argument begins after asking what reasons a person has, including testimony and experience where relevant. It does not assume that Christian confidence lacks evidence. The demonstrator represents the particular proposal of adding confidence without adding a reason.

The dialogue is invented. The hand crank is a visual analogy, not a claim that people can freely choose precise beliefs or that Frank advocates evidence-free certainty.

Frank stands at a pamphlet table on the left, addressing Phil and Ruth beside the Carousel of Certainty. Greg, Jonathan and Morgan listen behind them. Ruth turns the right-hand crank on a cream, raspberry and gold cabinet labeled CONFIDENCE BOOSTER. Its MY CONFIDENCE pointer is near the high end between 0% and 100%. The separate COMMITMENT switch remains at NOT YET. Phil points into the REASONS tray, where the same two blank cream cards sit. Elaborate carousel horses, visitors, lights and foliage fill the setting.

Frank: Gather round! Christianity offers more than a theory to admire. I believe the resurrection has good evidence, and faith means trusting Christ enough to respond. You cannot spend your whole life standing outside scratching your head.

Phil: Does faith mean making a choice yourself while uncertainty remains, or becoming more certain that the resurrection happened? Those reflect different moves. This booth can move a needle, but what new reason has entered the tray?

Ruth: I’m a Christian, and I want to understand that difference too. Look: one turn makes the needle soar. But the reasons haven’t moved. Does faith exceed the strength of the reasons?

Notes from the queue · 02

Two controls, two questions

Greg’s distinction does real work. People must often act before every uncertainty is settled. The costs, benefits and alternatives can matter to a decision without making the underlying claim more probable.

The carousel is a simple illustration of action under uncertainty, not a comparison of the stakes of a ride and a religion. The script challenges manufactured evidential confidence, not the mere existence of trust, hope or commitment.

Committing under uncertainty is conceptually coherent; this scene does not establish that every particular commitment is wise. Greg’s question-led approach includes permission to acknowledge uncertainty.

The MY CONFIDENCE dial is well below its 100% endpoint. Phil, at right, gestures toward the control area. Two blank cards remain in the REASONS tray beneath the CONFIDENCE BOOSTER header. Greg: What do you mean by faith? Trusting Christ need not mean claiming that doubt is impossible. You can choose to rely on someone without resolving every question first. A decision still has to be made.

Ruth: Like boarding the carousel: I either get on or stay here. I don’t have to believe that nothing could possibly go wrong. So my commitment can be definite while my confidence remains less than complete.

Phil: Exactly. That switch can say yes without forcing the needle to the end. Commitment changes what you do. It doesn’t, by itself, add to the evidence.

Notes from the queue · 03

The family setting

Ruth and Greg identify why trusting a caring parent can be reasonable. Morgan challenges the further inference from an adult’s conviction to the truth of a religious claim, especially when the same method produces incompatible convictions.

Her final question carries the conversation forward: what could justify the part of faith that exceeds the evidence? The question is whether this way of arriving at a belief warrants the degree of certainty it produces. Testimony can carry evidence without carrying a guarantee.

Morgan’s criticism is a challenge within the dialogue, not a blanket narrator claim that every reliance on parents or testimony is irrational. Greg and Morgan explicitly narrow it in scene 004. A belief’s origin alone does not prove it false.

The confidence pointer remains below 100%, the commitment switch is down at YES, and the tray contains two blank cards. Ruth: My parents loved me, cared for me, and taught me that Jesus rose. As a child, trusting them was reasonable. Must every six-year-old become a historian before believing anything her family says?

Greg: No. Testimony can be evidence, and parents can pass on good reasons. The child’s lack of independent research doesn’t make everything she learns worthless. We all depend on other people for knowledge.

Morgan: I’m not a believer, and I agree. But loving families teach incompatible religious claims. Trusting parents or community leaders is demonstrably a poor way to determine what is true. To the degree that faith exceeds the evidence, to that degree it is irrational, right?

Notes from the queue · 04

How certain should we be?

Morgan’s challenge concerns a method: inherited conviction can produce strong belief in incompatible claims, so that conviction alone cannot decide among them. Greg’s reply prevents that criticism from turning into a rejection of every form of trust or testimony.

The strength of a belief and the support for it are different things. Someone can be sincerely certain and mistaken, or cautiously correct. Rational confidence should reflect the quality of the reasons, and those judgments can be revised. The added confidence is the part the argument challenges.

Parents and other informants can be reliable sources of evidence. Morgan’s broad wording in the previous scene is qualified explicitly here: the target is an unsupported guarantee, not all interpersonal trust. Degrees of belief need not be assigned exact numbers.

Greg holds the TRUST token at left. Morgan stands beside him and points to the unchanged REASONS tray. Phil stands at right beside three cards arranged MAYBE, PROBABLY and ALMOST CERTAINLY. The CONFIDENCE BOOSTER cabinet retains its non-maximal pointer and YES switch. Greg: Trusting parents or community leaders isn’t automatically irrational. Their reliability can give us evidence. But how does their certainty justify ours when equally trusted people teach incompatible things?

Morgan: It doesn’t, by itself. That’s the problem I mean: treating inherited conviction as a guarantee. Confidence can explain why we believe something without showing that we should believe it that strongly.

Phil: And certainty isn’t the only alternative to disbelief. Maybe, probably, and almost certainly are different judgments. Each needs appropriate support. Where faith adds confidence beyond that support, the extra confidence is unjustified.

Notes from the queue · 05

The number is an illustration

Frank’s willingness question can be relevant to a conversation, but it does not itself answer Ruth’s question about justified confidence. She has already distinguished an intention to act from how strongly she presently believes.

The number supplies a subjective example, not a measured probability or research result. A subjective assessment can still be criticized as poorly supported; personal ownership does not make every estimate equally reasonable. Exact percentages are optional. Honest judgment should remain open to correction upward or downward.

Frank’s documented willingness move explicitly allows exceptions. Ruth’s question about redemption raises her concern; the scene does not claim that Christianity requires a particular numerical confidence for salvation. Her choice to follow and her justified degree of confidence remain distinct.

Phil replies from the right with his notebook open. Four balloons read across the upper pair and then the lower pair, with Ruth speaking twice. Ruth: Suppose I study the resurrection and find myself less certain than before. Call it sixty-five percent, just for illustration. I might still follow Christ. Must I recover my childhood certainty to be redeemed?

Frank: Sometimes reluctance is about willingness, not missing information. That isn’t everyone’s situation. If you became convinced Christianity was true, would you act on it?

Ruth: I’ve said I would. My question is how certain I should claim to be now.

Phil: Her answer concerns commitment; her question concerns confidence. And sixty-five isn’t a measured resurrection probability. It illustrates a subjective assessment that may later need revision up or down.

Notes from the queue · 06

What does faith add?

Jonathan gives the positive objection its force: several reasons may support a conclusion more strongly together. The argument does not require a single decisive proof. It does require avoiding double-counting and weighing what the combined support actually establishes.

Phil’s question concerns the additional step after that assessment. If faith means another experience, testimony or reason, examine its evidential value. If it merely licenses stronger conviction without further support, it has not made the belief more rational. The handle extends farther than the reasons do.

A strong Christian case remains possible. This conversation does not adjudicate its contents or assign it a probability. The target is faith as a free-standing confidence multiplier, not faith defined as reliance or commitment.

Ruth holds an unattached gold and red crank extension marked EXTRA CONFIDENCE. The cabinet retains its CONFIDENCE BOOSTER header, YES switch, two blank cards and non-maximal pointer. Jonathan: A Christian can have several good reasons for strong belief, not just one. Several reasons together may justify strong confidence even when no single reason settles everything. We should consider the whole case, without counting the same support twice.

Phil: Certainly. But once all that support has been weighed, what does faith add? If it supplies another reason, assess that reason. If it only makes us feel more certain, the evidential gap remains.

Ruth: Saying faith carries me the rest of the way leaves a question: what distinguishes that extra confidence from wishful thinking? A longer handle doesn’t put anything new in the tray.

Notes from the queue · 07

Evidence cannot arrive yesterday

Ruth offers a legitimate kind of consideration: participation can bring experiences that an observer has not had. Their evidential value depends on what they support, how strongly, and how well competing explanations fit.

Later evidence can justify a later increase in confidence. It cannot make an earlier evidential overstatement appropriate at the time it was made. That is different from condemning the original practical decision to participate, which may have been reasonable under uncertainty.

Ruth’s transformation is fictional. No natural or divine explanation is established here. Greg’s caution about treating subjective impressions as divine messages is preserved; he does not deny every possible divine communication.

Morgan holds a card marked NEW EVIDENCE? in LATER, while Greg points toward NOW. The CONFIDENCE BOOSTER stands to the right with commitment at YES, confidence short of 100%, and two blank cards in REASONS. Ruth: After committing to Christ, I became more patient and found a community that helped me change. Isn’t that experience evidence too? Perhaps I needed to step inside before discovering something I couldn’t see from outside.

Greg: It deserves consideration. But I wouldn’t label every inner feeling a message from God. Ask what actually happened and why it supports that explanation. An experience should be examined, not merely given a religious name.

Morgan: Yes. Compare divine action with community support, expectation and other explanations. Genuine new evidence can raise confidence afterward. It cannot travel backward and justify overstating your evidence before you had it. That ticket has a date.

Notes from the queue · 08

The inspection travels

Incompatible claims cannot both be true in the same respect. Their conflict does not identify the winner; it exposes the need for a method that can distinguish a well-supported conclusion from a sincerely held error. Faith-generated certainty alone supplies no such distinction.

The comparison also applies to other religions and secular convictions. Common scrutiny need not yield equal confidence or equal conclusions. Any difference should be supported by reasons rather than a special exemption for one’s own view.

Different traditions and adherents are not interchangeable. Frank offers reasons for Christianity; this scene tests whether faith alone is a reliable way to select among incompatible claims, without determining which is true.

Frank gestures toward the discussion from behind his pamphlet table. Morgan holds one magnifying glass between equal-sized cards labeled CHRISTIAN CLAIM and MUSLIM CLAIM. Ruth holds a loosened red velvet cord beside the cards. The cabinet stands at right with CONFIDENCE BOOSTER above MY CONFIDENCE, a YES commitment switch, and the two unchanged reason cards. Frank: Different religions make incompatible claims. Christianity has reasons to examine; I’m not asking you to choose whichever experience feels strongest. A Christian and a Muslim can both be sincere without both being right about Jesus.

Morgan: Then how does faith itself distinguish them? If the same method can make either believer certain, their certainty cannot decide which claim is true. We need reasons that favor one conclusion over another, with the same scrutiny for both.

Ruth: That includes mine. Equal scrutiny doesn’t mean equal evidence or equally likely conclusions. But if a method could certify incompatible answers, confidence alone cannot be its deciding test. There's no special line for my ticket.

Notes from the queue · 09

The proposed divine preference

Frank can reasonably challenge a picture of faith that excludes relevant revelation or personal evidence at the outset. The response allows such reasons to be considered; it asks whether any additional confidence remains justified once all relevant support has been assessed.

The claim about a truth-valuing God is a conditional philosophical argument, with premises a Christian can discuss. It is not knowledge of divine psychology or a disproof of Christianity. Honest assessment may support high confidence; humility does not require permanent indecision.

The standard concerns the quality of reasons, not whichever confidence level is emotionally comfortable. The divine-preference conclusion remains conditional and contested.

Frank: Christianity calls for faith in God, not endless hesitation. If God really has revealed himself, why assume he wants everyone hovering at whatever confidence level feels comfortable? Revelation could give us good reason for full conviction.

Phil: It could. My argument is conditional: if God values truth, why would he prefer confidence beyond what our evidence and reasons support? I’m not claiming access to God’s motives, or proving Christianity false. I'm asking why any God would value a confidence that runs ahead of the evidence.

Ruth: Then the question isn’t whether confidence must stay low. It’s whether I should claim more than I can support. If I’m asking for truth, tampering with the needle isn’t an answer.

Notes from the queue · 10

A handle that works both ways

Jonathan applies the standard to the questioning side as well. A method that permits only declining confidence would be biased too. Honest assessment must remain open to stronger reasons and serious counterevidence.

Ruth retains her commitment without claiming certainty that the discussion has not earned. The central conclusion is that faith, treated as an independent confidence multiplier, cannot reliably distinguish incompatible claims. Where reasons support a belief, those reasons justify its confidence. The new sign leaves room for correction.

Assessment can be qualitative, fallible and disputed. Neither the dial’s final position nor agreement about a method settles Christianity’s truth. Genuine supporting evidence and downward revision both remain possible.

Jonathan: Then your method must work both ways. If good evidence supports Christianity, confidence should rise. If evidence weakens the case, it should fall. Careful scrutiny cannot mean keeping doubt at a fixed point regardless of what we learn.

Phil: Agreed. Good supporting evidence should raise our confidence; serious contrary evidence should lower it. But faith, in that it constitutes surpassing the evidence, is an inappropriate and dangerous tool for honest seekers.

Ruth: I’m still choosing to follow Christ. I can do that without claiming every question is settled. Let’s call this the Confidence Adjuster. Apparently the deluxe model has a handle that works both ways. The degree of belief is tied to the degree of the evidence for those who are rational.

The handle works both ways.
